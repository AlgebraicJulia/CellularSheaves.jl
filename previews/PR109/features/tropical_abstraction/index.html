Tropical Abstraction: Coarse Grids for Optimal Control

This page explains the min-plus ("tropical") side of the package (TropicalAbstraction):

  • coarse grids for deterministic optimal control;
  • a layered controller built on them;
  • the certificates that come with that controller.

It covers three things: the idea, how it reads as sheaf theory, and how it relates to the literature it borrows from. The studies that measured these constructions, including where they do not pay off, accompany the paper on path lifting.

In one sentence: the multigrid ingredients we use for the HJB equation, the pushforward along an aggregation and its Galerkin coarse operator, still make sense when the semiring is $(\min, +)$. There is no subtraction, so the coarse solution cannot correct the fine one. Instead it bounds it. Lax and oplax morphisms of min-plus sheaves say which way each bound goes.

Optimal control as min-plus linear algebra

On a finite state graph whose edge $x \to y$ costs $W_{xy} \ge 0$, the cost-to-go to a target set $\Gamma$ solves the Bellman equation

\[V(x) = \min\Big(b(x),\ \min_y W_{xy} + V(y)\Big), \qquad b = 0 \text{ on } \Gamma,\ \infty \text{ elsewhere}.\]

In the semiring $\mathbb R_{\min} = (\mathbb R \cup \{\infty\}, \oplus = \min, \otimes = +)$ this is the linear fixed-point equation $V = W \otimes V \oplus b$, solved by the closure $V = W^* \otimes b$ with $W^* = I \oplus W \oplus W^{\otimes 2} \oplus \cdots$ (minplus_closure, cost_to_go). Dijkstra's algorithm computes one column of $W^*$. Other control problems are closures in other semirings:

semiring$\oplus$, $\otimes$closure computeshere
tropical$\min$, $+$minimum cost / minimum time to a targetcost_to_go, Abstraction
bottleneck$\max$, $\min$reach-avoid value: best worst-case margin along a pathreach_avoid, maxmin_closure
real$+$, $\times$discounted policy evaluation $V = P^* c$, $P = D^{-1}N$PathPackCoarsest in Multigrid

The first two are idempotent ($a \oplus a = a$), and that is what this page exploits. The third is the ordinary linear algebra of the HJB solvers (DoubleIntegratorHJB). It shows up here because the same factorization code (PathPack's chordal semiring LU) solves all three.

The running example in the studies is a minimum-time double integrator on a lattice:

  • States: $128^2$ positions times $13^2$ velocities, 2.77M states, of which 2.41M can reach the goal.
  • Moves: one step changes each velocity component by at most one, at unit cost.
  • Obstacles: two walls with gaps force the routes to snake.

The sheaf picture

The state graph as a min-plus sheaf

Let $\psi : X \to H$ send each state to a cell of a coarse graph $H$. In the example $H$ is the position grid and $\psi(q, v) = q$ forgets the velocity (projection_homomorphism). Pushing the state graph forward along $\psi$ gives a min-plus sheaf $F$ on $H$:

  • the stalk over a cell $q$ is $\mathbb R_{\min}^{\psi^{-1}(q)}$, a value for every state in the fibre (for $(q, v) \mapsto q$, a function of the velocity);
  • the map on an edge $q \to q'$ is the block $W_{qq'}$ of $W$ from the fibre over $q'$ to the fibre over $q$, and moves inside a cell are loops.

Two caveats on the word sheaf. The edges are directed (controls are not reversible), so strictly $F$ is a representation of the directed cell graph in min-plus semimodules. And the maps are not restriction maps into an edge stalk but transfer maps between vertex stalks. We keep the package's usage because everything below is the min-plus counterpart of a construction on the real sheaves elsewhere in the package, and the analogy guides the constructions.

Sections become sub- and supersolutions

On a real sheaf a global section satisfies an equation on every edge. In $\mathbb R_{\min}$ the natural notion is an inequality, and there are two of them:

  • a lax section (subsolution): $V \le W \otimes V \oplus b$;
  • a colax section (supersolution): $W \otimes V \oplus b \le V$. This is exactly a control Lyapunov function: from every state off the targets some move lowers $V$ by at least its cost (is_control_lyapunov).

The optimal value is the greatest subsolution and the least supersolution, and a fixed point (the only one when there are no zero-cost cycles). Every subsolution lies below it and every supersolution above it:

\[\underbrace{V_{\text{sub}}}_{\text{lower bound, A* heuristic}} \;\le\; V^\star \;\le\; \underbrace{V_{\text{super}}}_{\text{upper bound, certified policy}} .\]

The vectors $\overline{\mathbb R}^X$ form a complete lattice and the Bellman operator is monotone, so this is Tarski's fixed-point theorem. That puts the construction in the setting of Ghrist and Riess's sheaves of lattices, whose global sections are fixed points of the Tarski Laplacian.

Pushforward and pullback: a Galois connection

Aggregate the states along a map $\mu$ (into blocks, or into modes of each stalk, below). The refinement $E = \mu^*$ copies a block's value to its states (tropical_pullback, refine). Its residual, the coarsening $P$, takes the minimum over each block (tropical_pushforward, coarsen). They form a Galois connection:

\[E g \le f \iff g \le P f, \qquad P E = I, \qquad E P \le I .\]

The real block average needs a division; here $P E = I$ holds with no normalization because $\min$ is idempotent. $EP$ is the max-plus projection of Cohen, Gaubert and Quadrat, the one used by max-plus finite elements. The Galerkin product $W_c = P \otimes W \otimes E$ is the quotient graph (quotient_graph): one vertex per block, the cheapest edge between two blocks, and edges inside a block contracted.

Lax and oplax morphisms give bounds in opposite directions

A min-plus matrix $\Phi$ between two systems commutes with their Bellman operators up to the order (intertwines):

  • oplax when $\Phi \otimes W_s \le W_t \otimes \Phi$;
  • lax when $W_t \otimes \Phi \le \Phi \otimes W_s$;
  • strict when both hold.

Each kind of morphism carries one kind of section to the same kind:

coarse graphquotient rule$E$ is$E$ carriesso $E V_c$ is
optimistic ("may", ∃∃)$\min_{x \in B} \min_{y \in C} W_{xy}$ (quotient_graph)oplaxsubsolutions to subsolutionsa lower bound, an admissible A* heuristic (lower_bound)
pessimistic ("must", ∀∃)$\max_{x \in B} \min_{y \in C} W_{xy}$ (robust_quotient)laxsupersolutions to supersolutionsan upper bound and a control Lyapunov function (upper_bound)

The proofs are one line each.

  • Optimistic: if $V_c \le W_c V_c \oplus P b$, then $E V_c \le E W_c V_c \oplus E P b \le W E V_c \oplus b$. This uses the oplax square and $EP \le I$.
  • Pessimistic: if $W_c V_c \oplus b_c \le V_c$ with $b_c$ the largest terminal cost in each block, then $W E V_c \oplus b \le E W_c V_c \oplus E b_c \le E V_c$.

Neither square commutes strictly unless the moves are constant on blocks. The gap between the two quotients measures how much the aggregation hides.

The lifting defect: when the optimistic bound is exact

lifting_defect makes that gap precise, arc by arc. For an arc $B → C$ of the optimistic quotient, the defect is the worst excess, over the states $x$ of $B$, of the cost of reaching $C$ from $x$ over the coarse arc's cost:

\[ε(B, C) = \max_{x ∈ B} \big( d(x, C) − W_c(B, C) \big)^+ .\]

The arc lifts when its defect is zero: every state of $B$ can follow it at no extra cost. The path-lifting theorem says:

  • Exactness. $E V_c = V$ for every target that is a union of blocks if and only if every arc lifts.
  • Sandwich. Adding the defects to the coarse arc costs gives an upper bound, $E V_c ≤ V ≤ E V_c^{+ε} ≤ E V_c^{∀∃}$, never worse than the pessimistic quotient.
  • Two-sided error. The worst error of the lower bound at a block is at least its largest defect.
  • Optimality. The least sound upper weighting is the one-sided Hausdorff distance $\max_{x ∈ B} d(x, C)$ between blocks; the defect-inflated costs approximate it locally.

Every value lifting_defect returns is an upper bound on the true defect (a local scope or a search horizon only enlarges it), so these bounds stay sound.

Truncated pushforwards: keeping modes of the stalk

Forgetting the velocity entirely (one value per stalk) gives a weak bound, because a cell's cheapest state may be moving the wrong way. TruncatedPushforward keeps a partition of each stalk into $K$ modes (heading × speed bins of the velocity). The coarse sheaf $G$ then has stalk $\mathbb R_{\min}^K$ over every cell, and $E$, $P$ act stalk by stalk over the identity of $H$. With one mode it is the velocity-blind quotient; with one mode per state it is an isomorphism. The modes interpolate between the two, and the bound tightens monotonically.

Changing the gauge

A change of gauge relabels the zero point of cost at every state. It leaves the fine problem alone and changes what a coarse problem can see.

The operation. Pick a number $\varphi(x)$ for every state, the potential, and replace each edge cost by its reduced cost

\[W^\varphi_{xy} = W_{xy} + \varphi(y) - \varphi(x), \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad W^\varphi = D^{-1} \otimes W \otimes D,\quad D = \operatorname{diag}(\varphi).\]

In min-plus, $D$ is an invertible diagonal matrix, so this is a similarity: an isomorphism of min-plus sheaves that shifts each stalk's values by $\varphi$. In cochain terms, the edge costs are a 1-cochain $c$ and the change is $c \mapsto c + \delta\varphi$: it adds a coboundary.

Why the fine problem does not change. Along any path $x_0 \to \cdots \to x_n$ the potentials telescope:

\[\textstyle\sum W^\varphi(\text{path}) = \sum W(\text{path}) + \varphi(x_n) - \varphi(x_0).\]

Every path between the same endpoints shifts by the same amount, and every cycle by nothing. So the optimal paths, the optimal policy and the reachable set are unchanged. With $\varphi = 0$ on the targets, the cost-to-go shifts by exactly the potential: $V^\varphi = V - \varphi$. Only differences of $\varphi$ matter, which is why the name gauge fits.

The same move is familiar under other names:

  • A* is Dijkstra in a gauge. Dijkstra on the reduced costs with $\varphi = h$ is exactly A* with heuristic $h$. The heuristic must be consistent, $h(x) \le W_{xy} + h(y)$, precisely so that $W^\varphi \ge 0$.
  • Johnson's algorithm (1977) reweights a graph this way to remove negative edges.
  • Linear programming: $\varphi$ is the dual vector and $W^\varphi$ the reduced costs.
  • Reinforcement learning: potential-based reward shaping (Ng, Harada & Russell 1999), proved not to change the optimal policy.

Why the coarse problem does change. The pushforward takes a minimum over each block, and a minimum does not commute with adding different amounts to different states. So the same fine problem in two gauges has two different quotients.

A line of states $0 - 1 - 2 - 3$ with unit steps and the goal at 3 has $V = (3, 2, 1, 0)$. Aggregate it into blocks $A = \{0, 1\}$ and $B = \{2, 3\}$.

  • Original gauge. The quotient has one edge $A \to B$ of cost 1, and the steps inside each block are contracted away. The bound at state 0 is 1, against a true cost of 3.
  • Gauge $\varphi = V$. A step toward the goal costs 0 and a step away costs
    1. The coarse cost-to-go is 0 everywhere, and the bound is
    $\varphi + 0 = 3$, which is exact.

The gauge moves information out of the edge costs, which the minimum over blocks destroys, into the potential, which is added back state by state.

Abstraction(W, ψ; potential = φ) does this. The quotient is built from $W^\varphi$, and lower_bound returns $\varphi + E V^\varphi_c$, a refinement that copies block values on top of $\varphi$ rather than flat. The potential must be consistent, for example the cost-to-go of a relaxation, and then the bound is again a consistent heuristic.

This is the min-plus counterpart of aggregation with a near-nullspace mode in algebraic multigrid. There too the choice of basis decides what the coarse space must represent: TruncatedPushforwardCoarseSpace aggregates $D_h z$ rather than constants. Here the "mode" is $\varphi$, and the coarse space only represents corrections to it.

Elimination is min-plus harmonic extension

On a real sheaf, harmonic_extension fixes the values on boundary cells and solves for the interior. The min-plus counterpart is the Schur complement onto a separator $S$ (schur_complement):

\[W_S = W_{SS} \oplus W_{SI} \otimes W_{II}^* \otimes W_{IS}, \qquad (W_S)^* = (W^*)_{SS}.\]

The identity on the right says that eliminating the interior loses nothing on $S$; it holds in any closed semiring (Lehmann 1977). Cutting the state space into cells and eliminating every interior onto the portals gives an Overlay. The cells play the role of the cover in the Schwarz methods, and each shortcut is the harmonic extension of boundary data through a cell.

Two commutation facts organize the layered controller:

  • Restriction commutes with pushforward (base change). If $U$ is a union of fibres of $\mu$, restricting the fine graph to $\mu^{-1}(U)$ and then pushing forward equals pushing forward and then restricting to $U$. So the coarse bound stays admissible inside a corridor.
  • Aggregation and elimination commute only laxly. The two orders give $AE \le EA \le \text{exact}$, with equality when the aggregation is injective on the eliminated interior. Eliminating before aggregating is the tighter order. On the $64^2 \times 5^2$ lattice the two squares agreed on 46% of the entries, and the cost-to-go reached 0.961 (aggregate first) and 0.987 (eliminate first) of exact on average.

The layered controller and its certificates

LayeredPlanner splits control into two layers:

  • a planner routes over the coarse sheaf: cells, each with a mode of its stalk (plan_route);
  • a low-level controller searches the full 4D state graph exactly, but only over the pullback $\mu^{-1}(U)$ of a corridor $U$ around the route (corridor, layered_path). $U$ contains the cells within Chebyshev distance width of the route, with modes within mode_width steps.

$U$ is a union of fibres, so base change keeps the coarse lower bound admissible inside it. If the corridor holds no path, the controller widens it.

certified_path adds two certificates computed per query, neither of which needs the optimum:

  • Stability (the lax side). The low level follows the shortest-path tree of $V_U$, the cost-to-go restricted to the corridor. is_lyapunov checks that $V_U$ is a Lyapunov morphism for the closed loop, in the discrete setting of categorical Lyapunov theory (Ames, Moeller & Tabuada; Ames, Mattenet & Moeller). Concretely, it is positive definite and decrescent, its decrease is strict, and the domain is closed under the closed loop. Every state above the corridor that can reach the target does so within $V_U$ steps.
  • Performance (the oplax side). With the lower bound $E V_c$ from an optimistic abstraction, the ratio $V_U(x) / E V_c(x)$ is a guaranteed bound on the closed loop's cost relative to the optimum:

\[E\,V_c^{\text{opt}} \;\le\; V^\star \;\le\; V_U .\]

The controller is sandwiched between an oplax and a lax morphism, and the width of the sandwich is its suboptimality certificate.

We also tried transporting a Lyapunov function from a pessimistic coarse sheaf (upper_bound). It is sound but conservative. A ∀∃ coarse edge needs every state in a mode to make the move, and wide speed bins rarely satisfy that. The per-query corridor certificate is the practical form of the lax side.

Relation to the literature

The ingredients are classical. What the sheaf view adds is a single vocabulary in which the heuristic, the coarse grid, the abstraction, the Lyapunov function and the elimination are the same few constructions:

  • pushforwards and their residuals;
  • lax and oplax morphisms;
  • harmonic extension;
  • base change.

Path problems and dioids. Shortest paths, bottleneck paths and transitive closure are one algebraic path problem over a closed semiring (Carré 1971; Lehmann 1977; Tarjan 1981; Gondran & Minoux 2008). Gaussian elimination, and with it the Schur complement identity used above, works in that generality. PathPack's chordal semiring LU is a sparse, supernodal instance.

Max-plus linear systems and residuation. The Galois connection $E \dashv P$, the projection $EP$ and the order-theoretic replacement for subtraction come from residuation theory in idempotent semimodules (Baccelli, Cohen, Olsder & Quadrat 1992; Cohen, Gaubert & Quadrat 2004). The closest relative of our coarse problems is the max-plus finite element method (Akian, Gaubert & Lakhoua 2008). It projects the Bellman operator onto a max-plus subsemimodule with exactly this residuated projection and gets one-sided error bounds. McEneaney's max-plus methods (2006) use the same linearity to avoid grids altogether.

Aggregation in dynamic programming. Aggregating states and solving the aggregate problem is an old idea (Bertsekas & Castañón 1989). So are multigrid methods for discrete-time stochastic control (Chow & Tsitsiklis 1991). In the real semiring these give corrections or approximations. In the min-plus semiring hard aggregation gives bounds, which is why the coarse solution can drive an exact search instead of an iteration.

Abstraction heuristics and route planning. An optimistic quotient used as an A* heuristic is Hierarchical A* (Holte, Perez, Zimmer & MacDonald 1996). The same idea underlies pattern databases (Culberson & Schaeffer 1998), the all-pairs table, and merge-and-shrink abstractions in classical planning (Helmert, Haslum & Hoffmann 2007). The overlay is customizable route planning (Delling, Goldberg, Pajor & Werneck 2011), a cousin of contraction hierarchies (Geisberger, Sanders, Schultes & Delling 2008). Its preprocessing is the Schur complement.

Abstraction-based control. The two quotients are the may and must transitions of modal transition systems (Larsen & Thomsen 1988). The must abstraction is what symbolic controller synthesis refines (Tabuada 2009). Feedback refinement relations (Reissig, Weber & Rungger 2017) give conditions under which an abstract controller refines soundly. The lax square $W \otimes E \le E \otimes W_c$ plays that role here, stated as a morphism rather than a relation.

Lyapunov certificates and layered control. Verifying a controller by a Lyapunov function along a planned route echoes LQR-trees and funnel libraries (Tedrake, Manchester, Tobenkin & Roberts 2010). The planner/low-level split is a layered control architecture in the sense of Matni, Ames & Doyle (2024). The certificate is a Lyapunov morphism in categorical Lyapunov theory (Ames, Moeller & Tabuada 2025; Ames, Mattenet & Moeller 2025). There a Lyapunov function is a lax morphism into a stability object, and the lax/oplax sandwich above is its two-sided form for optimal control.

Sheaves. The real side of the package follows Curry (2014) and Hansen & Ghrist (2019): global sections, Laplacians, harmonic extension. Sheaves valued in lattices and their fixed-point "Laplacian" (Ghrist & Riess 2022) are the natural home for the min-plus constructions. There, sections become sub- and supersolutions and Tarski's theorem replaces linear algebra.

Limitations and open directions

  • Deterministic only. Expectations bring back $(+, \times)$ and with it subtraction, so stochastic problems belong to the real-semiring solvers (GridPolicyIteration with Multigrid).
  • Lattice dynamics. The state graph is a discretization with finitely many controls and a unit time step. The bounds are exact for the lattice, not for the continuous system.
  • Fibres are the bottleneck. Wide velocity fibres make the pessimistic quotient conservative and the overlay dense. Choosing modes adaptively, per cell and per query, is the obvious next step.
  • Choosing the abstraction (block sizes, $k$, modes) is manual. In planning, merge-and-shrink and counterexample-guided abstraction refinement automate it. The lax/oplax gap is a natural refinement signal: refine where the two quotients disagree.
